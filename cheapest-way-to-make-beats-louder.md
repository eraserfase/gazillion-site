Nobody buys a plug-in to fix an arrangement. The cheapest loudness in the session is already installed.

The cheapest way to make a beat louder is the stuff the DAW already ships. Gain stage properly, which costs nothing. Put a stock clipper or saturator on the drums to take the tops off. Put the stock limiter last and ask it to do a little. Loudness is mostly crest factor, the distance between the peaks and the average level, so shrink that distance and the track sits louder at the same ceiling. When the free tools run out, one saturation and clipping plug-in is the cheapest thing worth paying for, well under the price of a mastering chain. Buy the chain last, if ever.

## Why the beat is quiet in the first place

A peak meter reads the single tallest sample. Your ears read the average over time. The gap between those two numbers is crest factor, and it decides almost everything about how loud a finished track feels at a fixed ceiling.

The arithmetic is worth doing once. Say the beat peaks at 0 dBFS and averages 14 dB below that. Get the same material to average 10 dB below its peaks instead, and at the same 0 dBFS ceiling the average level has gone up by 4 dB. Nothing was turned up. The tall parts simply stopped being so much taller than everything else, which left room to raise the whole thing.

That is the whole game. Every loudness tool does some version of it: turn the loud parts down over time, reshape them instantly, or spread their energy into harmonics so the peak stops sticking out. Price has nothing to do with whether the idea works.

## What costs nothing at all

Start with the part that has no plug-in attached to it. Set every channel fader to zero, put a gain utility or clip gain at the top of each channel, and trim there until the loudest section peaks somewhere around -12 to -6 dBFS. Then balance with the faders. The full method is in [gain staging](https://gazillionindustries.com/gain-staging/).

This is unglamorous and it is the highest-value free move in the list, because everything downstream reacts to level. A saturator fed a hot signal and the same saturator fed a sensible one are two different sounds. A limiter handed a mix that already sums into the red spends its whole range undoing the mistake instead of adding level.

Two habits cost more loudness than any missing plug-in: mixing with a limiter on the master from the first bar, which hides the problem until it is structural, and dragging faders down to fix levels, which leaves every plug-in on the channel still getting slammed while the meter looks calm.

## The stock clipper, if the DAW has one

A clipper is the best value free tool for this job, and most producers never open theirs. It rounds or flattens the loudest peaks instantly, with no attack and no release, so it does not pump or dull the groove the way a compressor working hard does. Lower peaks mean you can raise the whole signal before it touches the same ceiling. The mechanism is in [soft clipping explained](https://gazillionindustries.com/soft-clipping-explained/).

Put it on the drum bus rather than the master. Drums own the tallest peaks in almost every beat, so that is where the crest factor is hiding, and clipping the drum group leaves the bass and the vocal out of it. Raise the input until the front of the kick stops getting louder while the body keeps getting louder, then back off a little. That moment is audible in about ten seconds and it is more reliable than any meter.

Two things to watch. Cymbals distort before kicks do, because high frequency content has the sharpest peaks relative to its energy, so if the kit turns harsh as it gets louder that is the hats meeting the clipper. And if the clipper has an oversampling switch, turn it on for anything bright.

**SOFT CLIP as the out stage.** The clipper rounds the peak rather than holding a ceiling over it.

## The stock limiter, doing less than you think

Every DAW ships a limiter, and every DAW's limiter is good enough for a beat. The reason yours sounds worse than the reference is rarely the code. It is being asked for eight decibels of reduction on a mix whose peaks are all kick, so it ducks the whole arrangement on every downbeat.

Limit last, after the clipper has already shortened the spikes. Ask for a couple of decibels on the loudest hits, not a wall of reduction. Level-match against bypass before deciding it helped, because louder always sounds better for the first four seconds and then stops being true.

If a beat needs the limiter pinned to sound competitive, the limiter is not the part that is broken.

## Most "not loud enough" is arrangement

This is the free fix nobody wants, and it beats every plug-in in this article. A busy arrangement is quiet because its energy is spread thin and its peaks are stacked. Three sounds playing the same rhythm in the same register sum into one tall peak and one muddy average, which is the worst possible crest factor.

Mute things. Stagger entries so the peaks land at different moments. Give the loudest element of the beat a bar where nothing competes with it. Space in an arrangement reads as loudness because the parts that remain get to use the whole ceiling. More on the drum side of this in [how to make drums hit harder](https://gazillionindustries.com/how-to-make-drums-hit-harder/).

Transients are the other half. A kick with a defined front end reads as hard at a lower average level, so shaping the attack often removes the need to push anything at all.

## The low end is where the headroom went

A sub note spends much of its cycle near its own peak, so low frequency content holds the meter up in a way that a hi-hat never will. When the kick and the bass occupy the same two octaves and hit at the same time, they sum, and that sum sets the ceiling for the entire track. Everything else in the mix then lives underneath a number that two elements created.

Fixes, all free. Pick which one owns the sub and high-pass the other. Move one of them so they are not hitting on the same beat. Check the phase relationship, because two low sounds slightly out of step can produce a peak larger than either of them alone. Keep the lowest content centered, since a wide sub eats headroom for nothing audible.

A mix with a clean relationship between kick and bass is louder before any processing than a mix with a messy one is after all of it.

## So where does paying actually help

After the free list, the returns come from tools that reduce crest factor with less collateral damage, and from not having to make the same compromise twice. A stock clipper will take peaks off. A dedicated saturation and clipping plug-in will take the same peaks off while adding harmonic content that makes the result read as weight rather than as a reduction, and it will usually handle the input level so you are not hunting for a working setting.

The order to spend in, if you spend at all:

- Nothing. Gain stage, clip the drum bus, limit gently, fix the low end.

- One saturation and clipping plug-in for the drums and the mix bus. This is the step that changes the most per dollar.

- A better limiter, much later, and only once you can hear what the stock one is doing wrong.

- A mastering chain of specialist tools. Last. Most beats never need it, and a chain bought early mostly gives you more ways to make the same mistakes.

Note what is not on that list: anything sold as one-click loudness. Those hand the whole job to a limiter, which is the part that punishes bad crest factor hardest.

## A free chain to try tonight

- Take the limiter off the master. Leave it off until step 6.

- Faders to zero, trim each channel until the loudest section peaks around -12 to -6 dBFS, then rebalance.

- Decide whether the kick or the bass owns the bottom, and high-pass the other one.

- Route the drums to a bus. Put the stock clipper or saturator on it and push until the kick's attack stops growing, then back off.

- Play the arrangement and mute anything that is not earning its space.

- Now put the limiter back on the master and ask it for a decibel or two.

- Level-match against the original and listen to the hats and the snare, not the loudness.

If that gets the beat where it needs to be, you have your answer about what to buy.

## Questions people ask

### What is the cheapest plugin to make beats louder?

The cheapest useful one is a single saturation and clipping plug-in for the drum bus and the mix bus, which is where nearly all of the crest factor sits. That is a much smaller purchase than a mastering bundle and it covers most of the distance. Before spending anything, get the free version of the job done: gain staging, the stock clipper, the stock limiter last.

### Can I get a loud master with only stock plugins?

Yes, for a beat. Stock clippers, saturators and limiters all reduce crest factor, which is the mechanism that makes a track sit louder at the same ceiling. Paid tools mostly do it with fewer side effects and less fiddling, which matters more on a full mix than on drums.

### Do I need a whole mastering chain?

Usually no. A chain of specialist plug-ins solves problems that appear after the mix is already good. A quiet beat is normally quiet because of gain staging, a kick and bass fighting over the same octave, or an arrangement with every peak stacked on the same beat. None of those are fixed further down the chain.

### Should I use a compressor or a clipper to get louder?

A clipper, for peaks. A compressor turns the gain down over time with attack and release, so when it works hard on a beat you hear the movement. A clipper reshapes each sample instantly with no gain movement, so it removes peaks without the pumping. Compression is for shaping the groove; clipping is for the tops.

### Why is my beat quieter than the reference track?

Almost always crest factor rather than a missing plug-in. If the beat peaks in the same place as the reference and still sounds smaller, the average level is lower, which points at stacked peaks in the arrangement, an untamed kick or a kick and bass summing into one tall spike.

### Is it better to clip before the limiter?

Generally yes. The clipper takes the fastest spikes off so the limiter has less to hold back, and the limiter's gain reduction stays small enough to be inaudible. Reversed, the limiter spends its range on peaks the clipper would have handled for free.

### How loud should a beat actually be?

Loud enough to survive next to what it will be played against, with peaks below the ceiling and nothing audibly crushed. Chasing a specific number on a loudness meter matters less than the gap between peak and average, because streaming platforms turn everything down to a common target anyway.
