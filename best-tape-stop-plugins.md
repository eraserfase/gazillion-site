The tape stop is the sound of a machine losing its grip: pitch and time sagging together as the reel slows, then either silence or a scramble back up to speed. Every producer wants one. Most reach for an automation curve and wonder why it sounds like a pitch bend rather than a machine.

The best tape stop plugins are the ones that slow pitch and time together, let you set how long the stop takes, and put the transport under a control you can play in real time rather than draw in afterwards. A DAW's stock pitch automation can approximate it; a transport-based plugin gets the sag, the dropout and the return.

## The short version

- **What to look for**: pitch and time slowing together, not pitch alone

- **Second thing**: a stop length you can set, in time or in seconds

- **Third thing**: a return, so the track comes back in sync

## Why most tape stops sound wrong

On a real machine the tape itself slows down. Everything recorded on it slows with it: the pitch falls because the tape passes the head more slowly, and the tempo falls for exactly the same reason. The two are locked, because they are the same fact. That is covered properly in tape speed and pitch.

A pitch-shift plugin breaks that lock. It drops the pitch and leaves the timing alone, so the hits stay on the grid while the note sags, and the ear hears a synthesizer rather than a machine. This is the single most common mistake, and it is audible immediately on drums, where the grid is the thing you are listening to.

The second giveaway is the shape. A hand-drawn automation line falls in a straight line. A reel does not. It has mass, and mass means it loses speed fastest at the start and then crawls the last part of the way down, which is why a real stop has that long droop at the bottom before the sound dies.

## What a good tape stop plugin has to do

- Slow pitch and time together. Non-negotiable. If a plugin lets you change one without the other, that setting is not a tape stop.

- Let you set the length. A half-second stop is a stumble. A four-second stop is an ending. They are different effects and you need both.

- Come back. The stop is half of it. What sells it is the transport picking back up and rejoining the track in time, rather than the session limping along an eighth-note late for the rest of the song.

- Be playable. You want to catch the stop against a specific hit. A control you can hit, and automate afterwards, beats drawing a curve blind.

- Degrade like tape. As the speed falls the top end goes with it, and on a worn machine the level wobbles. Pitch alone is thin without that.

## The free routes, and what they cost you

You can get most of the way with what you already own. Bounce the section, drop it on an audio track, and use your DAW's varispeed or tape-mode stretch so pitch and time move together. It works, it sounds right, and it takes you out of the flow of the session and makes the move permanent.

The other free route is automating the transport-style pitch control that several DAWs expose on an audio clip. It is quick and it commits nothing, but the curve is straight unless you draw the droop yourself, and there is no return: you get the stop and then a hole.

## Who actually builds one in

Most tape plugins do not have a tape stop at all, because most of them model the tape rather than the machine. The ones that do tend to say so on the box: AudioThing's Reels, for instance, bills itself as a tape plugin with echo and a tape stop effect, and runs on macOS, Windows and Linux in every common format. Its trial goes silent for three seconds every forty-five and will not save, which is enough to judge a stop by.

Free physical models of tape machines exist and are excellent at the tape itself. CHOW Tape Model is one, released under the GPL, originally based on a Sony TC-260 and described in a paper presented at the DAFx conference in 2019. What it does not give you is a transport, because a reel-to-reel model is a model of tape running at speed.

## Tape stop on drums versus on a full mix

On a drum bus the stop is a rhythmic event. It works best short, caught against a snare, with the return landing on the downbeat of the next bar. Because the whole kit slows together, the room and the cymbals smear in a way a pitch shifter cannot fake.

On a full mix it is an arrangement move: an ending, a transition into a half-time section, a drop into a chorus. Longer stops suit it, and the loss of top end as the speed falls does most of the work. If you want the wobble on the way down as well, that is wow and flutter, and a slowing transport makes it worse in a way that is entirely correct.

## Getting one in time

A tape stop is a rhythmic event, and most of them land wrong because the length was chosen by feel rather than by the bar.

Decide first whether the stop should finish on a beat or run past it. A stop that completes exactly on the downbeat reads as tidy and slightly polite. A stop that is still falling when the next section arrives reads as a machine that was actually stopped, because that is what happens when somebody hits a button without consulting the tempo. The second is usually the one people are imagining.

Length then follows from tempo rather than from a millisecond value you liked once. A stop that takes about half a bar is dramatic and still musical at most tempos; a quarter of a bar reads as an effect; two bars reads as an ending. Because it is tempo-relative, a setting that worked at 90 will be wrong at 140, and this is the single commonest reason a stop that sounded great in one track feels off in the next.

The restart matters as much as the stop. Coming straight back at full speed is the giveaway, because a real transport had mass and took a moment to get there. The recovery is where most plugins are thin, and it is the part a listener registers without being able to name.

## Beyond the stop

The stop is the famous one, but a transport does four things and the other three are rarer and more useful once you have them.

The drag. Slowing without stopping, then recovering, which is the gesture that sounds like a hand resting on a reel rather than a button being pressed.

The catch-up. Having fallen behind, sprinting to rejoin where the music now is. This is the one that is almost never modeled, and it is the one that makes the machine sound like it is trying to keep up with something.

The reverse scrub. Pulling the head backward across the tape and letting it spring home, which is a performance you do with a hand rather than a setting you dial.

A plugin with only the stop gives you one punctuation mark. A plugin with the other three gives you something you can play across a whole arrangement, and that is a different category of tool.

## Doing it without a plugin

Every host can produce a tape stop, and knowing how makes it much easier to judge whether a dedicated tool is worth it.

The manual method is to automate the pitch or playback rate of a clip downward to zero over the length you want, with a curve rather than a straight line, because a real transport decelerates fastest at the start. That gets you most of the way for a one-off, and it is free.

What it does not get you is the recovery, the ability to perform it live, or the correct behavior when the audio is not a clip but a bus with several things arriving on it. The automation approach works on a rendered piece of audio and becomes awkward the moment you want the stop to happen to a whole mix in real time, which is exactly when people want it.

So the useful test for a dedicated plugin is not whether it can stop the tape, because anything can. It is whether the stop can happen live, on a bus, with a believable return to speed, and whether you can perform it repeatedly and get a slightly different result each time the way a hand on a machine would.

## Questions people ask

### What is the best tape stop plugin?

The one whose transport slows pitch and time together, lets you set the stop length, and returns to speed in sync. Plugins built around a transport do this; plugins built around a pitch control approximate it.

### Is there a free tape stop plugin?

There are free options, and your DAW can already do a version of it with varispeed or tape-mode stretching on a bounced clip. The paid difference is playability and the return, not the basic falling pitch.

### How do I make a tape stop effect in my DAW?

Bounce the section, place it on an audio track, and use a stretch mode that moves pitch and time together, then draw a curve that falls fast and flattens out at the bottom. The step-by-step is in how to make a tape stop effect.

### Why does my tape stop sound like a pitch bend?

Because the timing is not moving with the pitch. Use a varispeed or tape stretch mode rather than a pitch shifter, and let the top end fall away as the speed drops.

### How long should a tape stop be?

Set it against the bar rather than in milliseconds. Roughly half a bar is dramatic and still musical at most tempos, a quarter reads as an effect, two bars reads as an ending. Because it is tempo-relative, a setting that worked at 90 will be wrong at 140.

### Why does my tape stop sound fake?

Usually the restart rather than the stop. Coming straight back at full speed gives it away, because a real transport had mass and took a moment to reach speed. The recovery is the part listeners notice without being able to name it.

### Can I do a tape stop without a plugin?

Yes, by automating a clip's pitch or playback rate down to zero along a curve rather than a straight line. What that does not give you is a live performance on a bus, or a believable return to speed, which is the part listeners actually notice.
