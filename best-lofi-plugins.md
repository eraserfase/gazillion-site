## What people actually mean by lofi

Lofi is not a genre of plug-in, it is a set of limits that older equipment imposed and that everybody now adds back on purpose. Narrower bandwidth. A noise floor you can hear. Timing that is not perfectly locked. Distortion that arrives gradually rather than as a cliff. Each of those was a fault once, and each of them is now a control.

The reason this matters when choosing is that one plug-in rarely does all four, and the four are not equally important. Bandwidth and instability carry most of the impression. A bitcrusher on its own, with no noise floor and no speed movement, usually just sounds broken.

## The four jobs, and what to look for in each

- **Tape or cassette.** The workhorse. Look for speed instability as well as saturation, because the instability is what a stock saturator cannot give you. The difference is covered in [wow and flutter](https://gazillionindustries.com/wow-and-flutter/).

- **Noise.** Look for a filtered, level-controlled bed rather than a sample loop, and for something that is not identical in both channels, or it collapses in mono. See [tape hiss](https://gazillionindustries.com/tape-hiss/).

- **Bit depth and sample rate.** The digital half. Judge these on whether the aliasing is musical, because at low rates the aliasing is most of the sound.

- **Instruments.** Lofi keys, lofi drums, lofi guitar. These are sample libraries recorded through the character rather than plug-ins applying it, and they are a shortcut rather than a tool. Useful, but they do not help with the rest of your mix.

## Why a lofi chain often still sounds clean

Three reasons, in the order they usually apply.

The first is that the whole chain is doing the same job. Three plug-ins all rolling off the top end give you a duller track, not an older one. What sells age is different kinds of damage at once: less bandwidth *and* a noise floor *and* a grid that is slightly loose.

The second is that everything stays perfectly in time. Older equipment could not hold speed exactly, and the ear knows it. A mix that is bandwidth-limited and noisy but metronomically rigid still reads as a modern recording with a filter on it.

The third is level. Every one of these processes changes loudness, and louder almost always sounds better in an A-B. Match the level before judging, or you will keep choosing the setting that is simply hotter.

## Buying order, if you are starting from nothing

Buy the tape or cassette effect first. It covers bandwidth, noise and instability in one plug-in and it works on anything: drums, keys, a full mix, a bounced loop. Whatever you buy after that is filling gaps rather than doing the main job.

Second, if you work with sampled drums, is something that handles the digital side, so you have both halves of the sound available. Third, and only if the writing needs it, is an instrument library. The instrument is the least transferable purchase of the three, because the character is fixed in the recordings and you cannot apply it to anything else.

The readout is speed, not level. At **101.5%** the transport is running fast.

## The order to process in

Lofi chains go wrong in the order as often as in the settings, because each stage changes what the next one is looking at.

Pitch and speed effects belong first, while the material still has its full bandwidth. Anything that shifts speed resamples the signal, and doing that to something already filtered and noisy drags the artifacts along with it, so the noise ends up moving in pitch with the music, which nothing real does.

Bit and rate reduction next. These generate aliasing that lands at frequencies unrelated to the source, and a filter after them will tidy some of it away. Put the filter first and you are throwing away material before the stage that needed it.

Tape and noise near the end, because the tape is the medium and the medium is what everything was recorded onto. Noise last of all, or at least after the filtering, or it gets processed by stages that in the real chain happened before the noise existed.

That order also explains a common complaint. If a chain sounds harsh rather than warm, the bit reduction is usually after the filter instead of before it, so nothing is smoothing the aliasing.

## Lofi that still sounds like a record

The difference between a lofi track and a damaged one is usually restraint in three specific places.

**Keep one element clean.** A single bright, undegraded part gives the ear a reference, and everything else reads as deliberately aged rather than accidentally broken. Without it there is nothing to compare against and the mix just sounds low quality.

**Keep the low end honest.** Bit reduction and heavy saturation both do unpredictable things at the bottom. Check the kick and bass before and after, and if they have lost pitch definition, pull the processing off those parts and leave it on the rest.

**Keep the timing.** Speed effects are the most convincing part of a lofi chain and the fastest way to make a track unusable. A little wander over a bar reads as tape; enough to move a downbeat reads as a mistake.

**DRIVE at 0.** The medium, doing nothing to the level.

**DRIVE at 92.** Soft asymmetric saturation, oversampled, with the level held flat across the travel.

## Bit depth and sample rate, and what they actually do

These two get used interchangeably in lofi discussions and they do completely different things, which is why chains built on a vague understanding of them tend to sound harsh instead of old.

**Bit depth** sets how finely each sample's level is described. Reducing it makes the quiet parts grainy first, because the error is a fixed size while the signal is not. It produces a gritty noise that rides with the material and is at its most audible in fades and tails.

**Sample rate** sets how often the level is measured. Reducing it removes the top end, and anything above what the new rate can carry folds back down into the audible range at unrelated frequencies. That folding is aliasing, and it is what makes heavy rate reduction sound metallic and slightly wrong rather than simply dull.

The practical consequence is that these are two different textures and should be reached for separately. If the goal is grit and noise, reduce bits. If the goal is a narrow, clangorous, sampled-on-old-hardware character, reduce the rate. If the result is unpleasantly harsh, it is almost always the rate, and a filter after it rather than before it will tame most of the damage.

## Getting lofi without a lofi plugin

A lot of what people reach for a lofi plugin to do is already available in any host, and knowing that makes the plugins easier to judge.

Pitching a sample down and back up loses information on the way and is the oldest trick in this category. Recording something out to a speaker and back in through a microphone puts a real room and a real cheap transducer in the chain, which nothing models exactly. Bouncing through a lossy codec at a low bitrate produces a specific, recognizable set of artifacts. Printing a part to audio and editing it destructively, rather than keeping everything live and reversible, produces the small irreversible commitments that most old records are full of.

None of that is an argument against buying a plugin, which is faster and repeatable and does not require a microphone. It is an argument for knowing which part of the sound you are paying for, so you can tell whether a given plugin is actually giving you something you could not get in ten minutes with what you already own.

## Questions people ask

### What is the best lofi plugin?

For most people, a tape or cassette effect, because it covers bandwidth, noise and speed instability at once and works on any source. Instrument libraries are a faster route to a specific sound and a slower route to a whole mix.

### What is the best free lofi VST?

Free options exist in every one of the four jobs, and your DAW almost certainly ships a bitcrusher and a filter already. The gap in free tools is usually speed instability and a playable transport, which is where paid plug-ins earn the difference.

### How do I make my beats sound lofi?

Narrow the bandwidth without simply cutting the highs, bring a noise floor up under the quiet parts, and loosen the grid with a little speed movement. Doing all three lightly beats doing one of them heavily. The full chain is in [how to make music sound like a cassette tape](https://gazillionindustries.com/how-to-make-music-sound-like-cassette/).

### Is lofi just a low-pass filter?

No. A filter removes top end; a tape path makes the top end go soft while noise comes up underneath and the speed drifts. The filter is one quarter of it, and on its own it reads as a muffled modern recording.

### What order should lofi plugins go in?

Speed and pitch first, bit and rate reduction next, filtering after that, tape and noise near the end. If a chain sounds harsh rather than warm, the bit reduction is usually sitting after the filter instead of before it.

### Why does my lofi sound broken instead of warm?

Usually because nothing in the mix is clean. One bright, unprocessed element gives the ear a reference and makes everything else read as deliberate. Check the low end too, since heavy processing often takes the pitch out of the kick and bass.

### What is the difference between bit reduction and sample rate reduction?

Bit depth sets how finely each sample's level is described, so reducing it makes quiet passages grainy. Sample rate sets how often level is measured, so reducing it removes the top and folds what was above it back down as aliasing. Grit comes from bits; metallic clangor comes from rate.
