# The Minimoog Voyager

> The Minimoog Voyager's history: how Bob Moog won his name back, the 2002 launch, the dual filters and touch surface, hip-hop's Voyagers, and why one voice teaches.

Source: https://gazillionindustries.com/minimoog-voyager/  
Published 2026-09-20, updated 2026-10-28. By Gazillion Industries, who make JOURNEYMAN.

---

In 1970 Bob Moog put a synthesizer in a wooden case with a keyboard on it and no patch cables, because working musicians could not carry a wall of modules to a gig. Thirty-two years later, having lost and won back the right to use his own name, he did it again.

The Minimoog Voyager is a monophonic analog synthesizer designed by Robert Moog and shipped by Moog Music from late summer 2002, the first new Minimoog since the original went out of production in the early eighties. It keeps the three-oscillator, ladder-filter architecture of the 1970 instrument and adds a second filter, patch memory, MIDI, CV inputs and a touch surface. Production ended in 2015 after about 14,000 units. Moog called it the last synthesizer Bob Moog designed from start to finish.

## The short version

- **Shipped**: late summer 2002, designed by Robert Moog

- **Voices**: one, on a 44-key keyboard with velocity and aftertouch

- **Oscillators**: three, continuously variable waveform, plus noise and an external input

- **Filters**: two ladder filters: dual lowpass, or highpass into lowpass, with spacing

- **Memory**: 128 presets, expandable to 896

- **Launch price**: $3,495 signed, $2,995 standard; £2,999.99 in the UK

- **Discontinued**: announced 30 September 2015, about 14,000 made

## Losing the name

The original Minimoog was a practical answer to an impractical instrument. Modular systems cost tens of thousands of dollars and had to be patched by hand for every sound, so Moog's engineers hard-wired the most useful connections, bolted on a keyboard and made something a band could put in a van. It ran from 1970 into the early eighties and sold over 12,000 units, which for an instrument that plays one note at a time is a strange kind of triumph.

Then Moog lost the company. He had sold it to Norlin in 1971 and stayed on as a designer until 1977; in 1978 he founded Big Briar in the mountains of North Carolina and spent two decades building theremins and effects pedals while other people sold instruments with his surname on them. The original trademark lapsed in 1983. In Britain a man in Caerphilly registered the Moog Music and Minimoog names in 1996 and built his own "Welsh Minimoogs," which is why the Voyager was sold in the UK as "Voyager by Bob Moog" and nothing else.

Getting the name back took, in Moog's words, "three or four years," and there "was never a lawsuit." He and the trademark's American holder settled. Sound On Sound called the reacquisition the most significant development of 2002, and Big Briar became Moog Music the same year. The Voyager, shown as a non-working concept at the 2000 NAMM show, was the first instrument to ship under the restored name. The name itself came from a public competition. Moog later said that it won out over suggestions like "Moogabooga."

## What Bob Moog said he was building

"The main impetus behind it was to build a monophonic, performance-oriented synthesizer as good as we could." That is Moog in March 2003, and the sentence has three load-bearing words. Monophonic, because one voice is the whole point. Performance-oriented, because the instrument was meant to be played by hands in real time rather than programmed. And "as good as we could," which is a working engineer's phrase for an instrument with no excuses built in.

The filter started life as the classic Moog filter from the Moogerfooger MF-101 pedal, and his idea was two of them in parallel, one per output channel. The touch surface came from a Big Briar design that never shipped: a pad that reads where your finger is in two dimensions and how much of it is touching, so one gesture moves several things at once. Those two decisions are most of what separates the Voyager from the instrument it descends from.

## What is actually on the panel

Three oscillators with a waveform control that sweeps continuously from triangle through sawtooth and square into narrow pulse, tunable across a wide range, with oscillator three doubling as a modulation source. A noise generator and an external input, so you can run other instruments through the filters.

Two filters, which is the biggest musical change from the original. In dual lowpass mode you get two ladder filters, one per channel, with a SPACING control that offsets them from each other: a single sound with two different amounts of brightness in it, which in stereo reads as width and in mono as a subtle formant. In highpass-lowpass mode the same pair becomes a band, and spacing sets how wide it is. Two ADSR envelopes, one for the filter and one for the amplifier. An LFO from 0.2 to 50 Hz with sample and hold, and two modulation busses. Later firmware let each filter run at one, two, three or four poles; when Sound On Sound reviewed it in June 2003 the 12 dB mode was still a promise.

Then the modern parts: 128 patch locations expandable to 896, MIDI in, out and through, 14 CV and gate inputs on the back for anyone who wants to treat it as a module again, and the touch surface, which out of the box sends X to cutoff, Y to spacing and finger area to resonance. The touch surface is the part people either build their playing around or never use twice. The reviewer's verdict was "very positive" with "rather more niggles" than expected, and a warning that anyone buying on features per pound should look elsewhere.

**One voice, left to right.** Oscillators into the mixer, the mixer into two filters, the filters into an amplifier the volume envelope opens and closes.

## The variants

- **Signature Edition** (2002): the first 600, $3,495, signed.

- **Standard** (2002 on): $2,995. Sold in the UK as the Performer Edition at £2,199.99.

- **Anniversary Edition** (2004–05): black cabinet, backlit panel, $3,295.

- **Electric Blue** (2005 on) and the **Select Series** (2006 on), which let buyers specify cabinet and lighting.

- **Rack Mount Edition** (2005): five rack spaces, MIDI controlled, no keyboard and no touch surface. Daft Punk toured four of them in 2006 and 2007.

- **Old School** (2008–09): no memory, no MIDI, no touch surface. A 1970 argument in a 2008 case.

- **XL** (2010): 61 keys, a ribbon controller and a patch bay. The only version that survived the 2015 announcement, and it went in 2017.

- **10th Anniversary Gold** (2012): 31 units, 24-karat gold, $15,000.

## The end

Bob Moog died on 21 August 2005 in Asheville, of a brain tumor diagnosed that April, three years into the production run. The instrument outlived him by a decade. On 30 September 2015 Moog Music announced the end of Voyager production: thirteen years, about 14,000 instruments, a final 600 hand-built in the downtown Asheville factory. Trent Reznor, in the farewell video, called it "the archetype of what a synthesizer is and should be." Moog's own line was that the Voyager "is the foundation of the modern Moog synthesizer," which is company language for the thing everything since has been measured against.

## Hip-hop and soul

A Voyager that belonged to [J Dilla](https://gazillionindustries.com/j-dilla/) is in the Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture, donated by his mother in July 2014 along with his sampler. The museum's record puts the serial number at 263, inside the first run, dates it 2002 to 2005, lists its place of use as Los Angeles, and notes that he chose the walnut finish. The instrument is not in that case as a synthesizer. It is there as evidence about how a particular producer worked, and the record says only that: owned, used, Los Angeles. Nobody has his patches.

The other documented Voyager in this lane sits in Mike Dean's rig. Dean, who has produced and mixed for Kanye West, Travis Scott, Scarface, UGK, Beyoncé and Jay-Z, told Sound On Sound in 2022 that the Moog instruments he uses most are the Matriarch, the Grandmother, "and the Voyager for my signature bass sound," and that the Voyager is part of his live core setup. That is the Voyager's job in this music in one sentence: the bass. One oscillator an octave down, a second detuned by a few cents against it, the filter low and the envelope doing most of the work. A bass line that occupies the whole bottom of a record without a single dB of EQ.

What the two filters add to that is bite and weight from the same note. Let one pass a little more of the harmonics than the other and the bass reads on a phone speaker, where a single dark filter would leave a rumor. The woolly, rolled-off low end of the neo-soul records of the early 2000s lives in that neighborhood, though no public source puts a Voyager on a specific one of them, and this page will not either.

## Why one voice teaches

A polysynth forgives you. Play a chord through a pad and the voices cover for each other; a dull envelope or a lazy filter setting disappears into the stack. A monosynth has nowhere to hide. Every note is the whole sound, so every decision on the panel is audible, and the Voyager's panel is laid out as a lesson in the order those decisions should be made.

Start from the job, not the waveform. Deep sub, round bass, rubber funk bass, soft lead, clav, stab. Then give each oscillator a role: one for the body (triangle or a soft saw), one for articulation (saw, square, a moderate pulse), and a third that has to justify itself, as a quiet octave, a fifth or a modulation source. Three full-level saws is the sound of someone who has not decided yet.

Choose the filter slope before the cutoff. Four poles is dark, woolly and classic; three poles is rubber and melodic; two poles is clav and reed and open R&B lead. Then let the envelopes do the articulation, because a patch's identity usually lives in the filter attack and decay and the envelope amount, not in how many oscillators are on. Keep resonance structural rather than theatrical. Pick one movement, a slow waveform drift or a small detune beat, and at most one supporting one. And give every patch a single contradiction: a dark body with a bright 120 ms transient, a pure sub with a quiet octave rasp, a plucked filter under a legato glide.

The Voyager rewards that discipline because it exposes the lack of it. The mixer level drives the filter harder, so loud is also a tone decision. Velocity, aftertouch and the touch surface are three more hands on the same patch, which is why the difference between a beginner and someone who has lived with the instrument is audible in four notes. Those four notes are the lesson, and they transfer to any monosynth, hardware or software, that gives you the same decisions to make.

## Where it costs you

It plays one note at a time, it is heavy, and it costs more used now than several polyphonic instruments that will do more of the obvious jobs. Analog oscillators drift, so tuning is a habit rather than an event. The patch memory stores panel positions, which means recalling a patch leaves every physical knob pointing at the wrong value until you move it, and that trips people who came from digital instruments. The 2003 reviewer found the aftertouch "more like an on/off switch," and the touch surface rewards practice in a way that is off-putting for the first month.

Against all of that: it was built by the man who invented the form, to his own specification, and it sold slightly more units than the original ever did.

## Questions people ask

### What is special about the Moog Voyager?

It is the Minimoog architecture with a second filter, patch memory, MIDI and CV, designed by Robert Moog himself and built after he regained the rights to his name. The dual filters with their spacing control, and the three-axis touch surface, are the parts you cannot get on the original.

### What are the differences between the Voyager and the original Minimoog?

The 1970 instrument has one lowpass ladder filter, no patch memory, no MIDI and no CV inputs beyond the basics. The Voyager has two filters with a spacing control, 128 presets expandable to 896, full MIDI, 14 CV and gate inputs, a touch surface, and a velocity- and pressure-sensitive keyboard. The core sound is closely related; the control over it is not.

### When did the Minimoog Voyager come out?

It was shown as a concept at the 2000 NAMM show and shipped from late summer 2002, the year Bob Moog regained the Moog Music name. Sound On Sound reviewed it in June 2003.

### Is the Minimoog Voyager discontinued?

Yes. Moog announced the end of production on 30 September 2015 after thirteen years; the XL followed in 2017. Moog has since reissued the original Minimoog, so a new Minimoog has been available on and off, but not the Voyager.

### How many Minimoog Voyagers were made?

About 14,000 across all variants over thirteen years, which is slightly more than the original Minimoog managed in its own production run.

### Did J Dilla use a Minimoog Voyager?

Yes. His Voyager, serial number 263, is in the Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture, dated 2002 to 2005 and listed as used in Los Angeles. The record documents ownership and use, not specific patches or songs.

### Is the Voyager polyphonic?

No. It plays one note at a time, with low, high or last-note priority. The Rack Mount Edition could be stacked, up to sixteen units, for people who wanted polyphony badly enough to buy it sixteen times.
