## What a drum bus compressor does that a channel compressor does not

A channel compressor listens to one drum and controls that drum. A drum bus compressor listens to all of them summed and controls the sum. The difference sounds administrative and it is the whole category.

When the detector sees the whole kit, every loud element pulls everything else down with it for as long as the gain stays reduced. The kick lands, the hats duck a fraction of a decibel, the room duck along with them, and the gain walks back up in the gap. That coupling is the effect. Separate hits stop reading as separate recordings and start reading as one performance in one space, because they now share a single moving gain. The reason a [drum bus](https://gazillionindustries.com/what-is-a-drum-bus/) exists at all is to give you a place to do that.

The second thing it does is raise the floor. Compressing peaks and adding make-up gain lifts everything that was not a peak: the decay of the snare, the bleed in the overheads, the tail of the room. A kit with a high [crest factor](https://gazillionindustries.com/crest-factor/) has a lot of space under the peaks, and a bus compressor is the cheapest way to fill it. This is why a compressed drum bus reads as bigger and not merely quieter, which is the single most common thing producers report and the thing they cannot get from compressing each drum on its own.

The cost is that the coupling works in both directions. Put a compressor across the bus and the kick now modulates the hats. Whether that reads as groove or as a wobble depends entirely on the release time relative to the tempo, which is the section with the numbers in it.

## Best type of compressor for drum bus: VCA, FET, opto, vari-mu

Four gain-cell families turn up in the marketing copy for nearly every compressor plugin sold, and on drums they are not interchangeable. What follows is what each one does to a kit, in the order most useful to you.

### VCA

A voltage-controlled amplifier design gives you fast, accurate, fully adjustable timing. The attack can be genuinely fast, the release can be genuinely long, and the gain change tracks the control voltage without much of its own opinion. On a drum bus this is usually what you want. You are shaping timing, and timing is the one thing a VCA topology will give you exactly. Most digital bus compressors, including the console-style ones everybody reaches for, sit in this family. If you only ever own one, own this.

### FET

A field-effect transistor design is very fast and it distorts on the way. That distortion is the appeal. Attack times in the microseconds mean a FET-style plugin can grab a stick hit before the stick has finished, which flattens the transient and pushes the body forward, and the harmonic mess it adds while doing so reads as aggression. On a snare channel it is frequently the best compressor plugin for snare drum duty you will find. Across a whole bus it can be glorious or it can be a smear, depending on how much low end is hitting the detector.

### Opto

An optical design uses a light source and a light-dependent resistor, and the resistor takes its time. Attack and release are slow, program-dependent and largely out of your hands, which makes an opto plugin a poor first choice on a drum bus: drums are a timing problem and this is the topology with the least timing control. It works as a second, gentler stage catching long-term drift, or on a room mic you want to swell rather than snap.

### Vari-mu

A variable-mu design uses tubes whose gain falls as signal rises, so the ratio increases with level rather than sitting at a fixed number. Soft at the bottom, firm at the top. On a drum bus this is the least obvious compression of the four, because the onset is gradual and there is no single point where the sound changes. Wrong tool for a snare that is 6 dB too loud. Right tool when the bus is already balanced and you want density with no visible seam.

- **Punch and control**: VCA

- **Aggression and grit**: FET

- **Slow swell, no timing control**: opto

- **Density with no seam**: vari-mu

One caution about the taxonomy. Two plugins wearing the same topology label can be further apart than two wearing different ones. Use the family names to narrow the shortlist, then use your ears.

## The fork: control or character

Every argument about the best drum compressor plugin collapses into one question, and almost nobody asks it out loud. Do you want the compressor to control the drums, or do you want it to change them?

Control means the gain moves and nothing else does. Transients survive, the tone at the output matches the tone at the input, and nulling the processed signal against the dry one at matched level leaves only level. Transparent bus compression is a real engineering target and a hard one, and the plugins that hit it advertise the fact.

Character means the compressor adds harmonics, shifts the low end, softens the top, or all three, whether you asked or not. It is why a bus compressor can improve a loop while doing almost no gain reduction at all, which is a hint that the gain reduction was never the point on that particular plugin.

There is a third branch, and it is the one most people are actually on: you want neither decision, you want the drums fixed. A plugin built for one bus can make the control-or-character call once, at the factory, and hand you a single knob. That is a real trade and the section below puts it first, because it is what we build.

Neither of the first two is better. What matters is knowing which one you reached for, because they fail in opposite ways. A transparent compressor asked to add life will do nothing and you will keep turning it up until the kit is flat. A character compressor asked to control a wild bus will color everything long before it has caught the peaks, and you will end up with a loud, busy, still-uneven drum bus. Half the plugins people buy are the right plugin for the other job.

## Attack and release against tempo, with the numbers

Attack decides whether the drums punch. Release decides whether the bus breathes in time. Both are tempo problems, and the maddening part is that the same numbers give opposite results at different tempos, which is why copied settings so rarely survive the trip.

A quarter note lasts 60,000 divided by the tempo, in milliseconds. Three worked examples, with the sixteenth note included because on most drum programming the sixteenth is the shortest gap the compressor has to recover in.

- **90 bpm**: quarter 667 ms, eighth 333 ms, sixteenth 167 ms

- **140 bpm**: quarter 429 ms, eighth 214 ms, sixteenth 107 ms

- **170 bpm**: quarter 353 ms, eighth 176 ms, sixteenth 88 ms

Set the release so the gain is back near zero before the next hit you care about. At 90 bpm on a sparse boom-bap loop, the next hit you care about is usually the eighth, so 200 to 300 ms lands cleanly inside 333 ms and the compressor is idle again when the snare arrives. At 170 bpm with sixteenth hats, 200 ms means the gain never gets back up at all and the whole bus sits permanently pulled down, which is the flat, lifeless result people blame on the plugin. Same setting, opposite outcome, and the only thing that changed was the tempo.

Attack works the other way. Under about 3 ms and the compressor catches the stick itself, which kills the crack; the plugins that do this on purpose are the FET-style ones, and the grit they add is the compensation. At 10 to 30 ms the [transient](https://gazillionindustries.com/what-is-a-transient/) is already through the door before the gain moves, so the hit stays sharp and the body behind it gets squeezed. If your drums lost their punch the moment you engaged the compressor, attack is the first thing to check and it is right roughly nine times out of ten.

The sidechain high-pass is the control nobody mentions and it changes more than either of the above. Without it, a drum bus compressor is very nearly a kick-triggered ducker, because the kick carries most of the energy and the detector is an energy detector. High-pass the detector at 80 to 120 Hz and the snare and the hats start contributing to the gain decision. The kit suddenly sounds like a kit instead of a pump. Set it by sweeping up until the gain reduction meter stops moving in perfect lockstep with the kick, then leave it there. The full control-by-control walk is in [drum bus compressor settings](https://gazillionindustries.com/drum-bus-compressor-settings/).

## Best parallel drum bus compressor setup

Parallel compression lets you have the compressed sound without giving up the dynamics, which on drums is close to having it both ways. You keep the dry kit at full transient, and you blend a heavily compressed copy underneath it that carries the room, the decay and the density.

The old method is a send to a return track with a compressor on it. The modern method is a Mix or Dry/Wet control on the compressor itself, which every plugin named above has, and which is better in one specific way: the dry and wet paths are sample-aligned inside the plugin, so there is no chance of a delay-compensation mismatch smearing the transient. If you have ever set up parallel drums on a send and found the result oddly soft, that is the usual culprit.

Settings for the parallel copy are the inverse of the ones above. Go hard: 8:1 or higher, the fastest attack the plugin has, a short release, 10 dB or more of gain reduction. The point of the parallel path is to be squashed into a single dense block, because you are going to hide it under a dry signal that already has all the punch. Then bring it up from silence until the kit thickens, and stop about 2 dB before the point where it sounds good on its own. Our longer piece on [parallel compression](https://gazillionindustries.com/parallel-compression/) covers the routing in both directions.

On DRUGS that control is **MIX**, and there is no latency anywhere in the path, so the dry stays sample-aligned with the wet at every setting and parallel compression will not comb-filter on you. Take DOSE most of the way up, then pull MIX back until the dry kit is still the first thing you hear. The shot below is DOSE 95 with MIX at 45, which is an ordinary place to land on a break.

One check that saves an hour: listen to the parallel bus in mono. A heavily compressed drum copy is where stereo width problems become audible first, because compression raises the correlated and the uncorrelated content together and the room mics are the uncorrelated part. If the kit hollows out when you sum, pull the parallel level down before you start reaching for width tools.

## Best compressor plugin for kick drum and snare drum

Channel compression on individual drums is a repair, not a default. Before you put a compressor on a kick, ask what is wrong with it, because the answer is often a level automation problem or a sample choice problem and a compressor will make both worse.

When it is the right tool on a kick, you want the opposite of bus settings. Fast release, so the gain is back before the next kick; medium attack, 5 to 15 ms, to let the beater click through; a low ratio and shallow reduction, because a kick has little dynamic range to start with and squashing it makes it shorter rather than bigger. Any VCA design does this cleanly. A kick that needs to be bigger rather than steadier is a [saturation](https://gazillionindustries.com/how-to-saturate-drums/) job.

Snares are where channel compression earns its place, and where the best compressor plugin for snare drum question has a real answer: a fast FET-style design. The range between the hard hits and the ghost notes is enormous, and a fast compressor with a moderate release brings the ghosts up into audibility, which is most of what makes a programmed snare sound played. Expect 6 dB or more of reduction, far too much on the bus and normal on one drum. Watch the decay: too short a release and the tail flutters, too long and the ghosts never arrive.

Then take both off and listen to the bus again. Individual compression that sounded like an improvement in solo frequently disappears in the mix, and every stage you leave in is a stage the bus compressor has to work around.

## The ten-minute audition test

This settles which compressor belongs on your bus faster than any review, including this one. Do it with a loop you know intimately, not with a demo file. Start with ours, because it costs nothing and steps two, five, six and seven collapse into one knob; steps three, nine and ten are the ones that decide anything.

- Load the compressor on the drum bus with all other bus processing bypassed.

- Set ratio 4:1, attack 20 ms, release 200 ms, and lower the threshold until the meter reads 3 dB on the loudest hits.

- Adjust the output gain until bypassed and engaged measure the same loudness.

- Bypass and engage five times while listening to the hats and the room, not the kick.

- Set the attack to its fastest value and listen for the stick going soft.

- Return the attack to 20 ms and shorten the release until the bus pumps, then back off until it stops.

- Engage the sidechain high-pass and sweep it from 20 Hz to 200 Hz while watching the gain reduction meter.

- Drive the threshold down to 10 dB of reduction for fifteen seconds to hear the plugin's character with the politeness removed.

- Return to 3 dB, rematch the loudness, and play the loop on a phone speaker.

- Keep the plugin only if step nine sounded better than the bypass.

Step three is the one people skip and it invalidates everything after it. Louder wins every blind comparison for the first few seconds, and an unmatched level is responsible for a large share of the moments where a plugin sounds transformative in a demo and ordinary in a session. A [VU meter](https://gazillionindustries.com/vu-meter/) or any loudness meter across the bus makes the match a five-second job.

## What it costs

Bus compression is not free in any sense except the price. Four costs, plainly.

It takes away dynamic range, and dynamic range is what makes a fill feel like a fill. A bus squeezed hard enough to sound huge in an eight-bar loop turns relentless across three minutes, which you cannot hear while working on the loop and can hear instantly on the arrangement.

It moves the low end. Any compressor with a full-range detector is being driven mostly by the kick, so the perceived balance between the kick and everything else shifts as you increase the amount. The high-pass helps and does not eliminate it.

It makes mistakes further back harder to fix. Once the bus is glued, the individual drums are no longer independent, and a snare that needed to come down 2 dB now takes the whole kit with it when you move it. Balance first, compress second.

And it competes with the limiter at the end of the chain. Every decibel of crest factor you remove on the drum bus is a decibel the master [limiter](https://gazillionindustries.com/how-to-use-a-limiter/) does not get to remove, which is fine until both are working hard and the record stops moving at all.

### How much gain reduction should a drum bus compressor do?

Two to four decibels on the loudest hits for glue you feel rather than hear. Four to six is obvious and still musical on drums. Past six you are making an effect, which is a legitimate choice as long as you made it on purpose. If the meter never returns to zero you have stopped compressing and started limiting, and [the difference matters](https://gazillionindustries.com/limiter-vs-compressor/).

### Should I use one compressor on the drum bus or one on each drum?

Start with the bus. It is the stage that makes separate hits sound like one kit and it is the one you would miss if it vanished. Add channel compression only where a specific drum has a specific problem, and take it off again if the bus already solved it. Two stages of light compression usually beat one stage of heavy, but three stages is where mixes go to die.

### Why do my drums sound worse after I compress them?

In order of likelihood: the attack is too fast and is eating the transients; the level is not matched so you are comparing loud against quiet; the release is longer than the gap between hits so the gain never recovers; or there is no sidechain high-pass and the kick is ducking the entire kit. Work through those four in that order and the fourth one rarely survives.
