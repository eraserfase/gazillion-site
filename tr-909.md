# The TR-909

> What the TR-909 actually is: which voices are circuits and which are 6-bit samples, why the kick's TUNE knob is not a pitch control, and how accent lengthens a hit.

The machine that made house and techno is half analog and half sampled, and almost nobody can tell you which half is which. The kick's tuning knob does not set the pitch, either. It is worth knowing what you are actually turning.

The Roland TR-909 Rhythm Composer arrived in 1983 or 1984 depending on which source you trust, cost around $1,195, and about 10,000 were built before it was discontinued. Its voices are mostly analog circuits, with exactly three exceptions: the hi-hats, the crash and the ride are 6-bit samples stored in ROM. It was one of the first drum machines with MIDI, it sold badly, and it ended up as the rhythmic foundation of Chicago house and Detroit techno once it hit the secondhand market.

## Which voices are which

This is the most commonly garbled fact about the machine. Only three voices are sampled: the hi-hats, which share one ROM with an address range selecting open or closed, the crash, and the ride. Everything else is generated by circuitry, including the hand clap and the rim shot, which sound so much like samples that reviewers at the time remarked on it.

The samples themselves are 6-bit and heavily compressed to make the most of those bits, which is exactly why the 909's cymbals sound the way they do. That thin, grainy, slightly nasty crash is not a design flaw or a worn-out sample: it is the sound of a very small amount of data being asked to represent a cymbal.

## The kick, and the knob that lies

The panel says TUNE. The manual says it sets the pitch. Two independent analyses of the circuit say something different: what it actually controls is the decay time of a falling pitch sweep, roughly 30 to 120 milliseconds as the machine ships, and there is nothing on the front panel that sets the base frequency at all.

That explains something every 909 user has noticed without being able to name it. Turning TUNE up does not give you a higher note; it gives you a longer, more pronounced downward bend at the start of the hit, which reads as more pitch and more punch. ATTACK is the same kind of mislabeling: it sets the level of a click, a short noise burst at the front of the kick, rather than an envelope time.

If you are trying to program 909-style kicks in a plugin, that is the actual recipe: a low sine with a fast downward pitch envelope of a few tens of milliseconds, plus a separate click layer you can turn up and down.

## Accent is not just volume

The accent system works by sending a control voltage into the envelope circuits, which means an accented hit is not simply louder: it is longer, because the envelope capacitors get charged further. Some voices ignore accent entirely in certain respects, like the snare's pitch component.

That is why a 909 pattern with accents programmed in feels like a performance rather than a loop with some hits turned up, and it is why velocity alone in a DAW does not reproduce the effect. If you want it, you need velocity mapped to decay as well as level.

## Against the 808

The [TR-808](https://gazillionindustries.com/tr-808/) is fully analog and its voices ring and die on their own. The 909 is a hybrid, its kick is built around a pitch sweep rather than a ringing filter, and its cymbals are low-resolution digital audio. In practice the 808 gives you long tuned low end and the 909 gives you a shorter, punchier kick with a click, which is most of why one became the foundation of hip hop and the other the foundation of dance music.

## It also sold badly

Roughly 10,000 units, discontinued quickly, and in the years afterward they turned up cheap: one Detroit producer has described buying his out of a pawnshop for fifty dollars. By the mid-nineties they were several hundred pounds; today a serviced one is a few thousand dollars, and a unit with provenance has attracted five-figure offers. The pattern is identical to the 808's, one generation later.

The reissues and emulations are numerous and the current hardware versions are made by Roland itself, so the sound is no longer gated behind the original machine. What the original still has is the calibration drift of an analog kit and the exact quantization noise of those 6-bit cymbals.

## Questions people ask

### Is the TR-909 analog or digital?

Both. The kick, snare, toms, rim shot and hand clap are analog circuits. The hi-hats, crash and ride are 6-bit samples played from ROM.

### What does the TUNE knob do on the 909 kick?

Circuit analyses find it sets the decay time of a downward pitch sweep rather than the base pitch, roughly 30 to 120 milliseconds as shipped. The manual calls it pitch, which is why so many people describe it that way.

### What is the difference between the TR-808 and TR-909?

The 808 is entirely analog with voices that ring and decay naturally, giving a long tuned low end. The 909 is a hybrid with sampled cymbals and a kick built on a pitch sweep and a click, giving a shorter, harder hit.

### How many TR-909s were made?

About 10,000, over a production run of a year or two. It was discontinued because it did not sell.

## What to take from it

Three things you can use tonight without owning one. A 909-style kick is a sine with a fast downward pitch envelope plus a separate click layer. Accents should lengthen a hit, not just raise it. And the cymbals are supposed to sound cheap: if you are layering a pristine crash over a 909 pattern, you are removing the part that makes it a 909.
