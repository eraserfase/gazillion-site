# How to chop a drum break

> How to chop a drum break: finding an exposed break, matching tempo, slicing at transients, keeping the swing, and making the chopped kit sit in a beat.

Four bars of drums on a 1969 B-side, six seconds long, cut because the horns needed somewhere to sit out. Nobody in that room thought they were making the most reused piece of music in the world.

To chop a drum break, isolate a section where the drums play alone, set your project tempo to the break's tempo, slice it at every transient, and map the slices across pads or keys so you can replay the pattern instead of looping it. Then trim each slice to a zero crossing or a short fade so nothing clicks, and rebuild the groove by hand. The chopping is mechanical. The rebuilding is where the beat comes from.

## The short version

- **Find**: bars where the drums play with nothing over them

- **Tempo**: match the session to the break before you cut anything

- **Slice**: at transients, one hit per slice

- **Clean**: zero crossings or 2–5 ms fades at every edge

- **Play**: map to pads and perform it, do not draw it

- **Keep**: the bleed and the room: that is half the sound

## Finding a break worth chopping

A break is the part of a record where the band drops out and the drummer keeps going. The canonical ones are canonical because they are clean, short and swung: the four bars from The Winstons' "Amen, Brother" in 1969, Clyde Stubblefield's bars on James Brown's "Funky Drummer" in 1970, the opening of The Honey Drippers' "Impeach the President" in 1973. What they have in common is exposure. No horns, no vocal, no bass, so the hits arrive with only the room behind them.

You will not always get that. When the break has bleed from the rest of the arrangement, you have two choices: filter around it, or use the bleed as texture and build the beat so the tail of a horn stab sounds deliberate. The second one usually sounds better than the first.

## How to chop a drum break

- Find the tempo of the break and set the session to it, so your grid means something.

- Cut the section you want, starting exactly on the first kick.

- Slice at every transient: kick, snare, hat, in that order of importance.

- Trim each slice so it starts at a zero crossing, or put a 2 to 5 ms fade on the front.

- Put a short fade on the tail of any slice that cuts off mid-ring.

- Map the slices to pads or keys, lowest to highest, in the order they occur.

- Play the pattern in by hand, then quantize lightly if at all.

- Duplicate the bar and change one hit so the loop does not feel like a loop.

## Keep the swing you paid for

The reason those four bars still work is that a person played them slightly off a grid, and slicing them to a grid is the fastest way to throw that away. Quantize to a swung grid if you quantize at all, or leave the slices where your fingers put them and fix only the hits that are obviously late.

If the break's tempo is not your tempo, decide whether you want the pitch to move with it. Repitching the whole break drags it up or down in key and changes the character of the kit, which producers have used deliberately since sampling time was expensive. Stretching keeps the pitch and adds artifacts. Time stretching explained covers which one to reach for.

## Make it sit

A chopped break usually arrives with too much information: hi-hat wash, a room, and whatever else bled in. Take the top down before you take the low end out, add weight with compression, and layer a modern kick under the sampled one if you need the bottom to reach a phone. Layering also lets you keep the sampled kick's attack while borrowing somebody else's sub; see how to layer kicks.

## Where it costs you

Chopping is destructive. Every slice edge is a place where the room stops abruptly, and twenty of them in a bar turns a live kit into a machine gun, which may be what you want and is usually not. Heavy quantizing does the same thing to the feel. And a famous break is famous: anyone over thirty will name it in two bars, so either flip it hard enough to be yours or accept that you are in conversation with everyone else who used it.

## Questions people ask

### How do you chop a break?

Match your session tempo to the break, cut it at the first kick, slice at every transient, clean the slice edges with zero crossings or short fades, map the slices to pads, and replay the pattern by hand rather than looping the original bar.

### How do I get the drums out of a song?

Look for a passage where the drums are already alone, which is the only way to get them clean. Failing that, stem separation tools can pull a drum track out of a full mix, with artifacts that get worse the busier the arrangement is. Filtering alone cannot separate instruments that overlap in frequency.

### What is the most used drum break?

The four-bar break from The Winstons' "Amen, Brother", released in 1969 as a B-side, which ran through hip hop, jungle and drum and bass for decades. "Funky Drummer" and "Impeach the President" are close behind.
