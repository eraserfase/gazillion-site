Tape on a drum bus does a specific thing: it thickens the hits, rounds the top, and puts a small amount of instability under the groove so the loop stops sounding stamped out by a machine. On drums, more than anywhere else, the instability is the point.

## Why drums show tape more than anything else

A drum hit is a transient followed by a decay, and both halves react to tape. The transient meets a medium that cannot follow it exactly, so the attack softens and the body gets denser. The decay picks up the noise floor, which is why a break through tape sounds like it is sitting in a room rather than in a file.

Then there is the speed. Sustained material hides small speed variation; a synth pad drifting a few cents is atmosphere. Drums do not hide it, because the grid is exactly what you are listening to. A hi-hat pattern through a machine with a little wow and flutter stops being a metronome, and that is usually the difference between a loop that sounds programmed and one that sounds played.

## Where to put it

On the drum bus, after whatever you use to glue the kit together and before the mix bus. Compression first, then tape: you want the tape reacting to a kit that is already moving as one thing, not flattening the compressor's work on the way in.

On individual hits it is usually a waste, with one exception. A sampled break you have chopped and rebuilt often has each slice sitting slightly too clean at the edges. Running the rebuilt loop through tape as one piece glues the slices back together, because they all pick up the same noise and the same speed wobble, which is what they would have had coming off a record in the first place.

## Settings that work on a break

- Start neutral. Get the tape in the path with everything low, and check the level match before you judge anything.

- Drive until it thickens, then back off a little. The point where a break stops sounding thin is usually a long way before the point where it sounds distorted.

- Add wear slowly. A small amount reads as a recording. A large amount reads as an effect. Both are useful, but decide which one you want.

- Set the speed movement by ear on the hats. They are the fastest thing in the loop and the first place instability becomes audible.

- Check in mono. Noise and modulation that sound wide can collapse, and drums are usually the most mono-critical thing in the mix.

- A-B against bypass at matched level. Tape makes almost anything sound better when it is also louder.

## Lofi drums, specifically

The lofi drum sound people are chasing is usually three things at once: a narrower bandwidth, a noise floor, and timing that is not quite locked. Tape supplies all three from one plug-in, which is why it turns up in every lofi chain.

The bandwidth is the part most people overdo. Rolling the top off a break makes it duller, not older; what makes it sound like a cassette is that the top end goes soft rather than disappearing, and that the noise underneath comes up to meet it. That is tape hiss, and the full chain is in how to make music sound like a cassette tape.

## Breaks, programmed drums and live kits

The three take tape completely differently, and a setting that suits one will usually embarrass another.

A sampled break has already been through a chain once, often including tape, so it arrives with its own noise and its own top-end limit. Adding much more stacks two sets of the same artifacts and the result gets cloudy fast. Keep noise low, use the saturation for weight, and let the existing character carry the age.

Programmed drums are the opposite case, and they are where tape earns the most. They are perfectly quantized, perfectly repeated and perfectly clean, so every behavior the tape adds is doing something the source had none of. This is the one place where pushing the speed instability harder than feels sensible is usually right, because the machine timing has no variation to compete with it.

A live kit already has air, bleed and a room in it. Tape on top mainly changes the top end and the noise floor, and the risk is losing the room rather than gaining a machine. Use less, and check the cymbals first — they are where a tape treatment on live drums shows its limits immediately.

## Tape and the low end

The single most common problem with tape on drums is what happens underneath, and it is worth checking deliberately every time rather than noticing it a week later.

Saturation generates harmonics above the fundamental, so a kick through a driven tape stage picks up content in the low mids that reads as more weight on small speakers and as less on large ones. It sounds bigger on a laptop and thinner on a system, which is exactly backwards from what most people are checking for.

Speed instability affects the bottom too. Pitch movement on a sustained low note is much more audible than the same movement higher up, so an 808 or a long kick tail will wobble conspicuously at settings that are invisible on the rest of the kit. If the drums need instability and the low end cannot take it, split them: age the top of the kit and leave the bottom alone.

## Parallel tape

Running tape in parallel rather than in series solves most of the objections to tape on drums, and it is underused because the category is usually discussed as an insert.

The method is a send to a bus with the tape stage on it, aggressively driven, returned underneath the dry drums. The transients stay intact because the dry path is untouched, and the weight, noise and saturation arrive underneath where they can be balanced to taste rather than committed to. If it turns out to be too much a week later, it is one fader.

Two details make the difference. Filter the return so the parallel path is not competing in the sub, where saturation harmonics tend to make a mess. And check the timing: if the tape plugin reports latency and the host is compensating, the two paths stay aligned, but if it does not report accurately, the parallel path can smear the transients it was supposed to leave alone. Test by nulling the return against the dry path with the tape bypassed.

## Drum bus order

On a drum bus, tape is usually competing for the same slot as the compressor, and which goes first changes the result more than either plugin's settings do.

Tape first means the compressor reacts to material whose peaks have already been rounded, so it works less hard and the result is smoother and less obviously compressed. Compressor first means the tape sees a signal that is already level, so its saturation is more consistent across the bar and the drums sound denser but flatter.

Neither is correct. The first is closer to how the material was originally made, since a compressor downstream of a real deck heard the tape. The second is easier to control. Worth trying both on the same session once, deliberately, so you know which one you prefer rather than inheriting whichever order you happened to build first.

## Questions people ask

### What is the best tape plugin for drums?

One whose speed moves, not just one that saturates. On drums the wow and flutter and the transport movement are what you can hear; the saturation is available from a dozen other plug-ins you already own.

### Should tape go before or after compression on the drum bus?

After, in most cases. Glue the kit first so the tape reacts to a kit moving as one thing. Putting tape first is a valid effect, but it softens the transients the compressor was going to work on.

### Does tape emulation make drums sound better?

It makes them denser, darker and less rigid. Whether that is better depends on the track. It is doing the opposite of what a transparent chain does, and on sampled breaks that is usually what you want.

### How do I get lofi drums?

Narrow the bandwidth without simply cutting the top, bring a noise floor up underneath, and add a small amount of speed instability so the grid loosens. Tape does all three at once.

### Should tape go on the drum bus or individual drums?

The bus, almost always. Part of the effect is everything arriving through one narrow window, and you only get that if the parts are already summed. Separate instances give you separate machines, separate noise beds and unrelated wobble.

### Does tape make drums sound bigger or smaller?

Both, on different speakers. Saturation adds harmonics in the low mids, which reads as weight on small speakers and can read as thinner on large ones. Check a kick on more than one system before committing.

### How much wow and flutter is right on drums?

Less than on anything sustained, except on programmed drums, where more is usually right. Machine-timed parts have no variation of their own, so instability is adding something the source genuinely lacks.

### Should I run tape on drums in parallel?

It solves most objections to tape on drums. A send to a driven tape bus returned under the dry kit keeps the transients intact and makes the weight a fader rather than a commitment. Filter the return so it is not competing in the sub.
