Mastering tape and lofi tape are the same technology pointed in opposite directions. One is trying to sound like a well-kept machine doing its job. The other is trying to sound like a machine that has been in a loft since 1994. The controls look identical and the settings are almost inverted.

A lofi tape plugin wants wear, noise and speed movement turned up, and bandwidth pulled in without simply cutting the highs. A mastering tape plugin wants the opposite of all four. Buying the second and trying to get the first is the most common mistake in the category.

- **Mastering tape wants**: saturation, stability, a clean floor

## The settings, inverted

**Wear.** A mastering chain wants a machine that was aligned this morning. A lofi chain wants one nobody has serviced. If a plugin has an age or wear control, this is the one to move first, because it usually moves several behaviors at once in the way they actually move together.

**Noise.** In mastering, the noise floor is something to keep under the music. In lofi it is part of the music, and it wants to be audible in the gaps. [Tape hiss](https://gazillionindustries.com/tape-hiss/) is the fastest single change you can make to a clean production, and it is also the one most people set ten decibels too low to hear.

**Speed.** Mastering work wants speed rock steady. Lofi wants it to wander. This is the behavior that no other plugin in your folder supplies, and it is what makes a bandwidth-limited noisy track read as old rather than filtered. [Wow and flutter](https://gazillionindustries.com/wow-and-flutter/) covers what the two halves of that movement sound like.

**Bandwidth.** Both want less top end, but they want it differently. In mastering, the loss should be gradual and level-dependent. In lofi it can be severe, and the important part is that the top goes soft rather than vanishing. A steep filter gives you a muffled modern recording; saturation of the highs gives you a worn tape.

## Why the reference matters more than the plugin

Most tape plugins are modeled on studio reel machines, because that is what professional engineers used. Those machines were fast, wide, quiet and stable, and their makers spent decades making them more so. A faithful reel simulation is therefore a fairly clean-sounding thing, and no amount of turning knobs on one will produce the sound of a cassette that has been played four hundred times.

A cassette is slow, narrow and consumer-built. Everything a reel machine was designed to avoid, a cassette does by default. That is why the reference is the decision, and it is made before you compare a single control. ## A lofi tape chain that works

- **Put it on a bus, not on every channel.** Instability that is independent on each track smears the timing rather than loosening it.

- **Set the wear by ear on the brightest source** in the mix, usually hats or a shaker.

- **Bring the noise up until you hear it in the gaps**, then back it off slightly. Audible is the target; obvious is too far.

- **Add speed movement last**, and less than feels right at first. It accumulates over a full track in a way it does not over four bars.

- **Check in mono**, because noise and modulation that sound wide can collapse or cancel.

- **Match the level and bypass.** If the bypassed version sounds better at matched level, you have gone too far, and that is a normal place to end up.

## Making it sit in a modern mix

The awkward part of lofi is that the record still has to compete. A track processed to sound like a worn cassette is quieter, duller and less stable than everything around it in a playlist, and the usual response is to undo the character with a limiter until it is loud and no longer lofi.

The way out is to be selective about what gets aged. Age the parts that carry the mood and leave the parts that carry the weight. Drums and bass can stay mostly intact while keys, samples, guitars and background layers go through the deck, and the result reads as a lofi record while still having a floor under it. This is closer to how the reference records were actually made than putting everything through one machine: the tape stage applied to some of it, at some point, not to all of it at once at the end.

The other half is the top end. Lofi processing takes away the frequencies that make a mix sound loud, so a lofi track at the same measured level sounds quieter than a clean one. Leave one element bright and unaged — a hat, a transient, a top layer — and the mix keeps a sense of level without giving up the texture everywhere else.

## Where the lofi ceiling is

There is a point past which more processing stops adding character and starts removing information, and it arrives sooner than most people expect. The signs are consistent: the bass stops having pitch and becomes a rumble, hats turn into noise bursts rather than metallic sounds, and any two takes start to sound identical because the differences between them have been processed away.

The useful test is memory. Play the aged version, then the original, then the aged version again. If the second pass through the aged version sounds thin rather than warm, the setting was tuned while your ears were adjusting to it rather than tuned to the material. This happens to everybody and it is the main reason lofi mixes tend to arrive over-processed.

Aiming low and stacking also beats aiming high in one pass. Two gentle stages in different places — some age on the sampler, a little more on the bus — sound more like a signal that went through several imperfect things than one heavy setting does, because a signal that went through several imperfect things is exactly what the references were.

**A well-kept deck.** AGE at nought, hiss at 8, barely any speed movement. This is the flattering setting and the one most cassette plugins ship at.

**The same machine, forty-eight years on.** Four things moved together — age, noise, bandwidth and instability. Turning any one of them up on its own does not get here, which is the whole point.

## Lofi tape on vocals

Vocals are the hardest thing to age well, because the ear has a lifetime of experience with what a voice should sound like and notices interference immediately.

The first problem is intelligibility. Most of what makes consonants legible lives in the range that tape processing softens first, so a vocal that sounded characterful in solo frequently turns out to be unintelligible in the mix. Check aged vocals against the arrangement rather than alone, and at a low level rather than a loud one, because quiet playback is where words disappear first.

The second is pitch. Speed instability that is charming on keys is distressing on a voice, because a voice is the one source where the listener knows exactly what the pitch was supposed to be. Keep instability substantially lower on vocals than on anything else, or apply it only to a duplicate layer sitting under the clean one.

That layering approach solves both problems at once and is how most records that sound like this were actually made. Keep the main vocal clean or nearly so, send a duplicate through the deck with everything turned up, and balance it underneath. The character is audible, the words survive, and you can change your mind about the amount later without re-recording anything.

## When it is finished

Lofi has no obvious stopping point, because there is no correct level of degradation the way there is a correct level for a vocal. Three checks work better than a setting.

**Play it after silence.** Leave the room, come back and play it cold. Ears adjust to processing within about a minute, and almost every over-aged mix was tuned by ears that had already acclimatized to the previous version.

**Play it after a reference.** Not to match it, but to notice which direction the difference runs. If your track is markedly darker and noisier than the records you are aiming at, that is worth knowing before mastering rather than after.

**Play it on a phone.** Lofi processing removes exactly the frequencies a small speaker relies on, so a mix that sounds warm on monitors can vanish entirely on a phone. If the track still communicates there, the processing is inside the limit.

## Questions people ask

### What is the best lofi tape plugin?

One modeled on a cassette rather than a studio reel, with wear, noise and speed instability as first-class controls. A reel simulation can be pushed toward lofi, but it starts a long way from it.

### Is there a free lofi tape VST?

There are free tape plugins that handle saturation and high-frequency loss well. The usual gap is convincing speed instability, which is the behavior that does most of the lofi work.

### Why does my lofi chain still sound clean?

Usually because every plugin in it is doing the same job. Three things rolling off the top give you a duller track. Age comes from different damage at once: less bandwidth, a noise floor, and a grid that is not perfectly locked.

### Should I use tape on individual tracks or the bus?

The bus, in nearly every case. Independent speed movement per track pulls the mix apart; shared movement on a bus keeps everything drifting together, which is what a single machine would have done.

### Why do my lofi tracks sound quiet next to everything else?

Because lofi processing removes the high frequencies that make a mix read as loud. The fix is not more limiting; it is leaving one bright element unprocessed so the mix keeps a sense of level, while the rest carries the texture.

### Should I age everything or just some parts?

Some parts, nearly always. Aging the elements that carry the mood and leaving the ones that carry the weight gives you a lofi record with a floor under it. It is also closer to how the reference records were made, since the tape stage applied to part of the signal chain rather than to the finished master.

### How do I know when I have gone too far?

When the bass stops having pitch and two different takes start sounding the same. Play the processed version, then the original, then the processed version again; if the second pass sounds thin rather than warm, you tuned it while your ears were adjusting.
