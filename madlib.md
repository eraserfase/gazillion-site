# Madlib

> How Madlib actually works: the hotel-room rig behind Madvillainy, sampling whole records, ten minutes a beat, and how the Quasimoto voice is really made.

Source: https://gazillionindustries.com/madlib/  
Published 2026-09-20, updated 2026-09-24. By Gazillion Industries.

---

A producer checks into a hotel in São Paulo in 2002 with a portable turntable, a cheap sampler and a tape deck, and comes home with the beats for a record people are still arguing about. His summary of the gear conversation: people think they need all this equipment.

Otis Jackson Jr., who records as Madlib, was born in 1973 in Oxnard, California, into a musical family, and has been the most prolific producer in independent hip hop for twenty-five years. His method is unusual in two ways: he samples whole records rather than hunting for one break, and he refuses to spend long on a beat. "I don't like to spend more than 10 minutes at a time on a beat," he has said. "I'm not like Dre; I don't take a week on a snare."

## The short version

- **Born**: 1973, Oxnard, California

- **Family**: father a musician, brother Oh No, uncle Jon Faddis

- **Key records**: Madvillainy, Champion Sound, Shades of Blue, Piñata, Bandana

- **Rig, famously**: portable turntable, an SP-303, a tape deck

- **Pace**: ten minutes a beat, and hundreds never kept

- **Mixdown**: straight to two channels, no stems

## The hotel room

In late 2002 he went to Brazil for a Red Bull Music Academy session and took almost nothing with him. The tracks he made there fed into Madvillainy, released in 2004 with MF DOOM, and his own account of the setup is specific: a portable turntable, a BOSS SP-303 and a little tape deck. He has repeated the point every time anybody asks about equipment.

That is not a story about minimalism as a virtue. It is a story about what a sampler is for. If your method is to find something in a record and get it into a loop quickly, the smallest box that can do that is the correct box, and everything else is a delay between hearing the thing and having it. Our piece on [the SP-303](https://gazillionindustries.com/sp-303/) covers what that particular machine did to whatever went through it.

## The whole record

Most sampling producers are looking for a break: one bar, one phrase, one chord. Madlib's description of his own practice is the opposite. "Every time I sample a record, I do the whole record," he has said — meaning he works through an entire album while it is in front of him, making beats out of everything usable rather than extracting one part.

The output of that method is volume, and volume is the point. Series like the Beat Konducta and the Medicine Show exist because the beats exist; they are published quantities of a process that never stops. His engineer's account is that hundreds of pieces get made and simply erased by the evening.

## Two channels, no stems

He mixes to stereo as he goes. "I'd lay the beat straight out. To two channels." There are no session files with thirty tracks waiting to be balanced later, which means every decision is committed at the moment it is made and cannot be revisited.

That creates a real engineering problem downstream, and there is a documented solution to it. Dave Cooley, the engineer who mixed and mastered a lot of this material, has described manufacturing stereo width out of Madlib's mono loops using gates, reverb and a signal generator — building a stereo field after the fact because the source did not have one. That is the cost of committing early, and someone has to pay it.

## The Quasimoto voice

The high-pitched character on his own records is not a pitch shifter. His explanation, given in several interviews over several years, is mechanical: he slows the tape down and raps slowly against it, so that at normal speed the voice comes back fast and high. The formants move with the pitch because the whole recording moved, which is why it sounds like a different creature rather than a processed human. See [formant shifting](https://gazillionindustries.com/formant-shifting/) for why that distinction matters and [varispeed](https://gazillionindustries.com/what-is-varispeed/) for the mechanism.

## The caveman and the iPad

In 2002 he said, "I don't use computers. I'm a caveman." By 2019 he was saying he made all of the beats for Bandana, his second album with Freddie Gibbs, on an iPad.

Both statements are true and the change is smaller than it looks. An iPad running a sampler app is the same object as a portable turntable and an SP-303: the smallest box that gets from hearing something to having it in a loop. The tool changed and the method did not.

## The catalog, briefly

Lootpack got him onto Stones Throw. Quasimoto gave him a voice to rap in. Yesterdays New Quintet let him play every instrument on a jazz record under invented band names. Shades of Blue gave him the Blue Note archive to rebuild. Champion Sound with [J Dilla](https://gazillionindustries.com/j-dilla/) in 2003 put two producers over each other's beats. Madvillainy with [MF DOOM](https://gazillionindustries.com/mf-doom/) in 2004 is the one most people start with. Piñata in 2014 and Bandana in 2019 with Freddie Gibbs proved the method still worked on records that needed to sell. Sound Ancestors in 2021, assembled with Kieran Hebden, is the closest thing to an edited overview of what he does.

## Questions people ask

### What equipment does Madlib use?

Famously a portable turntable, a BOSS SP-303 and a tape deck on the trip that produced Madvillainy material; samplers and turntables generally; and more recently an iPad, on which he says he made all the beats for Bandana.

### How does Madlib make the Quasimoto voice?

He slows the tape down and raps slowly, so the vocal plays back fast and high. He has said repeatedly that he does not pitch-shift the voice.

### How fast does Madlib work?

By his own account, about ten minutes on a beat before moving on, and he samples a whole record at a time rather than looking for one break. Most of what he makes is never released.

### How many records does Madlib own?

Nobody has published a number, including him. His own answers have ranged from a couple of rooms to four rooms and a wish for ten. Any specific figure you see is somebody's estimate.

## What to take from it

The transferable part is not the gear list. It is the refusal to treat a beat as precious: ten minutes, commit it to two channels, and go to the next one, which over twenty years produces a catalog nobody can survey. Most producers' problem is not that they lack tools. It is that they spend four hours on a snare and end the year with nine beats.

A hotel room, three objects, and a record people are still taking apart. The gear was never the variable.
