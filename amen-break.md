# The Amen break

> Where the Amen break came from, why this break and not another, the 1986 compilation that pitched it down, and the fact that nobody in the band was ever paid.

A band in Atlanta needed something for the back of a single in the spring of 1969. They cut an instrumental over a gospel melody, the horns dropped out for four bars, and the drummer played alone. Nobody in that room got paid for what happened next.

The Amen break is a four-bar drum solo by Gregory C. Coleman, starting one minute and twenty-six seconds into The Winstons' "Amen, Brother", the B-side of their 1969 single "Color Him Father". It runs six or seven seconds depending on who is counting, and it became the rhythmic foundation of hip hop, jungle and drum and bass after it was reissued on a compilation in 1986. Neither the drummer nor the copyright holder ever received a royalty for any of it.

## The short version

- **Record**: The Winstons, "Amen, Brother", 1969, a B-side

- **Drummer**: Gregory C. Coleman, who died in 2006

- **Where**: 1:26 into the track, four bars, six or seven seconds

- **How it spread**: a 1986 breaks compilation, pitched down to 33 rpm

- **Royalties**: none, ever, to anyone in the band

- **Now**: added to the National Recording Registry in May 2026

## Why this break and not another one

There are thousands of drum breaks on soul records and only one of them ended up underneath three genres. The reasons are musical and they are worth knowing if you are looking for your own.

Coleman rides a ride cymbal rather than a hi-hat, which fills the space differently: a continuous wash instead of a tight pulse, so a producer can put anything on top without fighting for room. The snare has a distinctly pitched tone, so it reads as a note as well as a hit. The recording itself has a crunch to it, the sound of a small room and a hot signal path. And the four bars are not identical, so a loop of the whole thing has internal variation built in rather than repeating one bar four times.

Who actually devised it is contested. Richard Lewis Spencer, the bandleader who held the copyright, said he directed it. Phil Tolotta, the other surviving member, said the solo was "pure GC". That disagreement has never been settled and probably cannot be.

## What tempo the Amen break runs at

Sixteen beats, and the whole thing is over in about seven seconds. That gives you the tempo without looking anything up: sixteen beats divided by the length in seconds, times sixty. Or the same sum the short way, 960 divided by the seconds.

Seven seconds flat gives 960 ÷ 7.0 = 137.1 BPM. Seven and a twentieth gives 136.2. Six point nine gives 139.1, and 6.8 gives 141.2. A tenth of a second at the edge of your selection moves the answer by two BPM, which is the whole reason no two sources agree on a figure. They are not arguing about the record. They are arguing about where the four bars start and stop.

Measure your own copy rather than taking anybody’s number, including that one. Drop the file in, put the loop brace on the first hit of bar one and the first hit of the bar after, read the length in seconds, divide 960 by it. What comes back is the tempo of your transfer, and your transfer is the only one your session has to agree with.

Then accept that the figure is an average. Coleman is a man, not a clock, and the four bars drift inside themselves. If bar four runs 1% slow against an average of 137 BPM, that bar is 17.5 ms longer than the grid says: 1,750 ms stretched to 1,767. Seventeen milliseconds at the end of a phrase does not read as an error. It reads as a lean, it is most of why the loop breathes, and it is the first thing you destroy by warping every bar flat onto the grid.

## The detail almost nobody mentions

What early hip hop actually sampled was not the record. It was the 1986 *Ultimate Breaks and Beats* compilation, and that version was pitched down from 45 rpm to 33 and a third. So the Amen that entered the canon was slower and lower than the one The Winstons played, and every producer who built on it was building on a copy that had already been altered. The break most people know is a second-generation object.

## What 45 to 33 and a third actually did

Play a 45 at 33 and a third and you are running it at 33.333 ÷ 45 = 0.7407 of its speed. One number does everything, because on a turntable tempo, pitch and length are the same quantity wearing three names.

Tempo first: 137.1 × 0.7407 = 101.6 BPM. Pitch: 12 × log₂(0.7407) = −5.20 semitones, a perfect fourth down and then twenty cents further. Length: 1 ÷ 0.7407 = 1.35, so every decay in the recording got 35% longer. A cymbal that rang for 400 ms on the single rings for 540 ms on the compilation, and the room behind it swelled by the same 35%. The break did not only get lower and slower. It got bigger, because the early reflections of that small room stretched along with the drums inside it.

None of that is a story about a machine. It is what a speed change does, on a turntable in 1986 or in your session this afternoon, and it is why a break that has been slowed always sounds like a larger room than the one it was cut in. [Tape speed](https://gazillionindustries.com/tape-speed/) and [varispeed](https://gazillionindustries.com/what-is-varispeed/) run the same arithmetic on other material.

A speed change moving pitch, tempo and length together, so the paragraph above has something to point at. These are small moves on purpose: the slowed file is −1.30 semitones and the 1986 drop was −5.20, almost exactly four times further (5.20 ÷ 1.30 = 4.0). Hear a quarter of it and multiply.

## What producers did to it

In hip hop it was mostly looped and slowed. In jungle and drum and bass it was taken apart: sliced into individual hits, re-sequenced into patterns Coleman never played, time-stretched to tempos the recording was never near, and layered against itself. That second treatment is the reason the break outlived its own genre twice. A loop is a borrowing; a chopped break is an instrument.

## How to squash the Amen break

Squashing the break means cutting its crest factor: pulling the loudest hits down, putting the same amount back, and letting the ride wash and the room come up underneath. The trade is better on this break than on most, because Coleman is on a ride and a ride never stops. Compress a tight hi-hat pattern and what rises is the gaps. Compress this and what rises is a continuous wash, which arrives as weight rather than as noise.

Two numbers tell you how far you went. Meter the loop and write down peak in dBFS and short-term loudness in LUFS, then subtract: a loop at −0.8 dBFS peak and −16.2 LUFS has a crest of 15.4 dB. Squash it, make the level back up, meter again, subtract again. The fall in that difference is the squash, in dB, and it is the only honest way to state it. [Crest factor](https://gazillionindustries.com/crest-factor/) has the long version.

Set the release off the grid rather than off a preset. At 137 BPM a sixteenth note is 60 ÷ 137 ÷ 4 = 109 ms. Compressor recovery is exponential, so one release time returns 63% of the gain (1 − 1/e) and three returns 95%. A 109 ms release is therefore two thirds back by the next sixteenth and all the way back three sixteenths later, at 328 ms. That surge repeats at 1 ÷ 0.109 s = 9.1 Hz, and 9 Hz is slow enough to follow and fast enough to hear as tremolo. Push the release out to a bar instead, 1,750 ms, and the loop sits down and stays down for the whole four. Both are usable. They are different records.

Attack is a frequency decision wearing a time label. Ten milliseconds is one full cycle of 100 Hz and ten cycles of 1 kHz, and the stick on that snare is mostly energy above 1 kHz, so a 10 ms attack lets the crack through and clamps the drum behind it. Drop to 0.1 ms, one cycle of 10 kHz, and the crack gets clamped with everything else. [What a transient is](https://gazillionindustries.com/what-is-a-transient/) covers the trade if it is new.

One more, and it is the one that gets missed. If the kick fundamental sits near 60 Hz, one cycle of it lasts 1,000 ÷ 60 = 16.7 ms. A detector with no high-pass on it follows that cycle, the gain ripples at 60 Hz, and the whole loop gets amplitude-modulated at 60 Hz. That puts a pair of sidebands 60 Hz either side of everything in the bar, and it turns up in the low mids as a grind no EQ will remove, because it is being manufactured downstream of the EQ.

### Do it

- Loop the four bars and set the session tempo to the figure you measured, not a round one.

- Meter the loop. Write down peak in dBFS and short-term loudness in LUFS, and subtract.

- Put one compressor on the drum bus with nothing else in the chain.

- High-pass the detector above the kick fundamental, 80 to 120 Hz.

- Set attack to 10 ms and release to one sixteenth note at your measured tempo.

- Push in until the meter reads 6 dB down on the loudest snare in the four bars.

- Add 6 dB of make-up gain.

- Meter again, subtract again, and write the new crest next to the old one.

- Bypass, match the two by loudness rather than by peak, and A/B them through a phone speaker.

Step nine is the one everybody skips and the only one that settles anything. Matched by peak instead of loudness, the squashed version is simply louder, and a louder version always wins. ## Where the squash sits in the chain

Squash the whole break and then chop it, and every slice carries the same gain envelope, so the slices glue to each other when you replay them. Chop first and squash the rebuilt pattern, and the compressor is reacting to your pattern rather than to Coleman’s: a ghost note you dragged forward now pushes the ride around in a place it never did. Both work. They produce different instruments, and the second one is the jungle answer.

Order against pitch matters more than people expect, and the arithmetic is tidy. Compress at the original speed with a 109 ms release, render, then slow the render to 0.7407 and the release stretches with everything else to 109 ÷ 0.7407 = 148 ms. Slow first, then set a sixteenth at the new tempo of 101.6 BPM, and you get 60 ÷ 101.6 ÷ 4 = 148 ms. The same number, because slowing the tape slows the pump along with it. The order changes the sound only if you skip the sum.

Saturation before the compressor lifts the quiet parts first, so the compressor sees a flatter signal and grabs less than the meter led you to expect. Saturation after takes the block you have already built and rounds the tops off it. Filtering before is the detector high-pass by another route, since it changes what the sidechain sees whether you meant it to or not. ## What goes wrong, and what each one sounds like

Six failures, each with its own signature. Learn the sound and you stop guessing at the cause.

**Tremolo on the cymbals.** Release too short for the tempo. The wash swells and falls between hits at the sixteenth rate, around 9 Hz, and you start hearing the swelling instead of the drumming. Lengthen the release past one sixteenth.

**Crack with no drum under it.** Attack too long. The stick gets through untouched while the body behind it is clamped, and the snare arrives as a rim with the shell missing. Shorten the attack, or take less.

**Cardboard.** Attack too short. The stick is clamped along with everything else, every hit loses its front, and four bars of one of the great drum performances turn up sounding like hands on a phone book.

**The bars stop differing.** Too much of it. The four bars of the Amen are not identical, and that is half of why the loop survived fifty years of repetition. If bar three sat 2 dB above bar one and you have taken 8 dB off the peaks, those two bars now land within a decibel of each other, and a loop that used to move just repeats.

**The bleed arrives.** A squash lifts everything sitting under the hits: the room, the tape noise, the last of a horn chord that had not finished decaying when the drums took over. Some of that is the sound. All of it is permanent once you print it.

**Grind in the low mids.** The detector following the kick fundamental, described above. High-pass the detector and it goes.

Then the general cost, which is a bill rather than a fault. A squash trades dynamic range for density and it spends most of that range in places a phone speaker cannot send you, the room and the bottom of the kick. Something that opens up beautifully over monitors can reach a phone with nothing added at all. That is why step nine ends where it does.

## Getting it into your session at the right speed

Every DAW offers the same fork under a different name: one algorithm that moves time and holds pitch, and one that moves both together. The second is what the turntable did in 1986, and for a break you are slowing a long way it is usually the better sound and always the simpler sum.

In Ableton Live the fork lives in the clip’s Warp Mode chooser. Re-Pitch ties speed to pitch and does exactly the arithmetic above; Beats holds the pitch and repeats or skips material at the transients, which is convenient at small moves and audible as stutter at large ones. Logic Pro’s Flex Time gives you the same pair, one algorithm that cuts at transients and holds pitch and one that acts as a speed control. In FL Studio it is the sampler channel’s stretch setting, with a resampling option that behaves as a speed change. [Time stretching explained](https://gazillionindustries.com/time-stretching-explained/) covers what each family does to a transient on the way through.

Then the trap that actually bites, which has nothing to do with warping at all. If your break file is 44.1 kHz, your session runs at 48 kHz, and something in the path plays the samples out without converting them, the break runs at 48 ÷ 44.1 = 1.0884 times speed. That is 8.8% fast and 12 × log₂(1.0884) = 1.47 semitones sharp, so a break you measured at 137 BPM turns up at 149. If a loop will not sit on the grid and sounds a semitone and a half high, stop warping and go and look at the sample rates.

## The money

Nobody in The Winstons was ever paid for the sampling. Spencer held the copyright to the catalog and never licensed the break; the records that used it simply used it, in an era before sample clearance was routine and then in a genre that could not have afforded it. Coleman, by Spencer's account, died in poverty in February 2006.

Spencer's own verdict, to The Economist in 2011, is the line to quote: "[Coleman's] heart and soul went into that drum break. Now these guys copy and paste it and make millions." He described the whole thing as plagiarism and said he came from an era when you did not steal people's ideas.

In 2015 two British DJs ran a crowdfunding campaign to give him something. The goal was £1,000. It finished at £24,000 from 1,811 donations, and the cheque reached him in November of that year. His reaction, to the BBC: "They didn't have to do that — I didn't even know them. Fifty years on, some young white boys that I've never met, halfway across the world said, 'We're going to give you a gift.' It's probably one of the sweetest things that's happened to me in a long time."

He had already won a Grammy, for the A-side. "Color Him Father" took Best Rhythm & Blues Song at the ceremony in March 1970. He retired from music in 1971 and died in December 2020, aged 78. Who controls the copyright now is not stated anywhere public.

## How many records used it

No honest answer exists, only dated ones, because every figure in circulation traces back to a single user-submitted database with no published methodology. The BBC reported 1,862 in November 2015. NPR said at least 2,000 in 2017. Mixmag said over 2,500 in 2018. Those are the numbers with a source and a date attached; anything quoted as a current total is a number somebody read off a website that afternoon.

The other thing that shaped how people think about it is not a record at all. In 2004 the artist Nate Harrison made "Can I Get an Amen?", a seventeen-minute-and-forty-six-second lecture delivered over a locked groove of the break itself, arguing that the whole history of the loop is a history of copyright and of who gets to own a piece of culture. It has probably done more to teach producers about sampling law than any legal writing.

## Questions people ask

### What is the Amen break?

A four-bar drum solo played by Gregory C. Coleman on The Winstons' 1969 B-side "Amen, Brother", lasting six or seven seconds, which became the most reused piece of recorded drumming in popular music.

### Who played the Amen break?

Gregory C. Coleman, the Winstons' drummer. He died in February 2006, aged 61, and never received royalties from the sampling.

### Is the Amen break copyrighted?

Yes. It was never public domain. The copyright sat with the band's leader, Richard Lewis Spencer, who died in 2020; nothing public says who holds it now. The fact that it went uncollected for decades is not the same as it being free.

### Why is the Amen break used so much?

A ride cymbal instead of a hi-hat, a snare with a pitched tone, a crunchy room, and four bars that vary rather than repeat. Plus a 1986 compilation that put it in every producer's hands at once, already pitched down.

### What tempo is the Amen break?

Divide 960 by the length of the four bars in seconds. Seven seconds gives 137 BPM, 6.9 gives 139, 7.05 gives 136. Measure your own copy: a tenth of a second at the edge of the selection is worth two BPM, and the drummer drifts inside the bars anyway, so the answer is an average.

### How long is the Amen break?

Four bars, six or seven seconds depending on where you set the edges. Sixteen beats, always, and the beats are the safer thing to count.

### What key is the Amen break in?

It has no key. The snare has a pitched ring and the toms have fundamentals, but there is no harmony in four bars of drums. If it has to sit with something, move the whole break by ear and let the snare ring land where it fits.

### How do you squash the Amen break?

One compressor on the bus, detector high-passed above the kick, attack near 10 ms, release set to one sixteenth note at the break’s measured tempo, pushed until the loudest snare reads 6 dB down, then 6 dB of make-up gain. Meter peak and loudness before and after; the drop in the difference between those two is how hard you squashed it.

### Why does my Amen break sound thin after compressing it?

Almost always the attack. Longer than about 10 ms and the stick passes untouched while the drum behind it gets clamped, which leaves a crack with no shell under it. Shorten the attack or take less gain.

### Can you get sued for using the Amen break?

In principle yes: a copyrighted recording that was never licensed for sampling. What has happened for fifty years is that nobody collected, which is a fact about enforcement rather than about permission. That is the record, not legal advice.

### Is the Amen break royalty free?

No. A pack containing a re-recording or a replayed version is a different object from the 1969 master, and only that pack’s own license tells you what you are allowed to do with it.
