# The TR-808

> How the TR-808 makes sound: the ringing circuit in every voice, what the kick's decay control really does, the transistor story, and how 808 became a word for bass.

Source: https://gazillionindustries.com/tr-808/  
Published 2026-09-20, updated 2026-09-24. By Gazillion Industries, who make BEEFY.

---

Hold a ruler against a desk, let it hang over the edge, and flick it. It rings at a pitch set by the overhang and dies on its own. That is not an analogy for the TR-808. That is how it works.

The Roland TR-808 Rhythm Composer was released in 1980 at a US list price of $1,195, sold poorly, and was discontinued after about three years and roughly 12,000 units. It generates every sound with analog circuits rather than samples, and the circuit at the heart of it is a bridged-T network: a filter that rings when you hit it with a pulse and decays by itself. It became the most influential drum machine ever built after it was already out of production and selling secondhand for under a hundred dollars.

## The short version

- **Released**: 1980, $1,195 US list

- **Discontinued**: after about three years, roughly 12,000 units

- **Sound**: analog circuits, no samples anywhere

- **Core circuit**: the bridged-T network, in every single voice

- **Kick**: a ringing filter around 50–56 Hz with a feedback loop

- **Now**: "808" usually means a tuned sub-bass, not the machine

## What it actually is

There are no recordings inside an 808. Every voice is built out of circuitry, and the same building block appears in all of them. A bridged-T network is a filter arrangement that, when you excite it with a short pulse, rings at its own frequency and fades out on its own. No envelope generator, no amplifier shaping the tail: the decay is the circuit running out of energy, which is exactly what a struck object does.

The split across the kit is worth knowing. The snare, the three toms, the three congas and the rim shot and clave use bridged-T networks as ringing oscillators. The handclap, cowbell, cymbal and hi-hats use them as band-pass filters instead, shaping noise rather than ringing. The formal analysis of the circuit, published at a digital audio conference in 2014, found the network "was used in every single one of its sound generators in some form".

## The kick, in detail

This is the voice everything else in modern music descends from, and it is widely described wrongly. It is not a sine oscillator into a filter into an amplifier. A pulse hits a bridged-T network that rings at a low frequency; a physical model of the circuit puts that around 49.5 Hz, while Roland's own tuning documentation gives 56 Hz. The ringing goes through a feedback buffer and then a tone and output stage.

DECAY is not an envelope control. It sets how much signal comes back around the feedback loop, which is why turning it up makes the note ring longer, and why past a certain point it approaches ringing indefinitely. TONE is a passive low-pass that shaves the click off the front. That is the entire kick: a ringing filter, a feedback path and a tone control.

There are also two separate pitch movements in that kick that get conflated. One is a jump in frequency during the first few milliseconds of the attack. The other is a slow downward drift through the tail, a sigh, caused by leakage in the circuit. Whether that second one was designed or is an accident of the components has never been settled in print.

## The faulty transistor story, handled carefully

The legend says the 808 sounds the way it does because Roland used defective transistors. The true version is smaller and more interesting.

There is a specific component: a rejected 2SC828-R transistor, marked by hand at the factory, sitting at position Q35 with only two of its three terminals connected. It works as the machine's white noise source. That noise feeds the snare, the handclap and the toms. It does not feed the kick, which has no noise generator at all.

The story's only original source is Roland's founder, Ikutaro Kakehashi, telling it on camera for a 2015 documentary, and the journalist who reported it at the time framed it as a claim rather than a finding. Roland later shipped substitute noise transistors when the rejected ones ran out. And the academic circuit analysis concluded that the architecture, not subtle component quirks, is what makes the machine sound like itself. So: a real part, a real story, and a much smaller role than the legend gives it.

## It did not sell

The 808 arrived in 1980 into a market that wanted machines to sound like real drums, and it sounded nothing like a kit. Sampled machines were the future and everyone knew it. Roland pulled it after about three years.

Whether that makes it a commercial failure depends on what you compare it to. Around 12,000 were built. The sampled machine that supposedly beat it, the LM-1, sold in the hundreds, and its successor a few thousand. What killed the 808 was not that nobody bought it; it was that it cost $1,195 and did not do the job the market said it wanted. By the mid-eighties they were changing hands for under a hundred dollars, which is the only reason a generation of broke producers in the Bronx, Detroit and the South ended up with one.

## The records

Two from 1982 did most of the work. Marvin Gaye's "Sexual Healing", released that October, put the machine on the radio in a context nobody expected. Afrika Bambaataa and Soulsonic Force's "Planet Rock", on Tommy Boy, made it the sound of a whole genre. There is a detail in the second one worth keeping: the people who made "Planet Rock" did not own an 808. They rented one.

What both records show is that the 808 was never trying to imitate drums, and the moment people stopped asking it to, it became useful.

## How "808" stopped meaning a machine

Southern hip hop is where the word changed. Producers took the kick, tuned it to a note, stretched its decay and used it as a bass line rather than a drum, and by the time trap became the dominant sound of American radio, "the 808" meant that tuned sub-bass whether or not a Roland machine was anywhere near it. It is now one of the few pieces of gear whose name became a common noun for a musical part.

## Two things that get repeated and should not be

- "Critics called it marching anteaters." That line comes from a November 1982 magazine review, it was written about a different machine entirely, and it was aimed at older organ rhythm boxes rather than at the 808.

- "The kick is a sine oscillator through a filter and a VCA." That describes a later machine. The 808 kick is a ringing bridged-T network with a feedback loop, which is why its decay control behaves the way it does.

## Questions people ask

### Why does the TR-808 sound like that?

Because every voice is a circuit that rings and dies on its own rather than a recording being played back. The decays are physical behavior, not envelopes, and the kick in particular is a low-frequency filter ringing inside a feedback loop.

### Was the TR-808 a failure?

It sold about 12,000 units in roughly three years and was discontinued because it did not sound enough like real drums for the market it was sold into. That is only a failure by the standard of what Roland wanted from it; secondhand, at under a hundred dollars, it did more for music than almost any instrument of its era.

### Did Roland use broken transistors in the 808?

One rejected transistor type was used as the noise source, feeding the snare, handclap and toms. It does not affect the kick. The story comes from Roland's founder in a 2015 documentary, and the published circuit analysis attributes the machine's character to its architecture rather than to that component.

### What does "808" mean in a song now?

Usually a tuned sub-bass part played as a bass line, descended from the machine's kick but produced by a sample or a synthesizer. The word survived the hardware.
